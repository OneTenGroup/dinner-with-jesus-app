# Dinner With Jesus 2.0 — Content Batch 05: Anger, Conflict, Forgiveness & Reconciliation

**Status:** Draft only. Nothing here has been written to `public.dinner_verses`. No production content was created, edited, activated, or deactivated. No application code was touched. Follows `docs/DWJ_EDITORIAL_STANDARD.md` (v.20) and the locked Batches 01-04.

**Scripture text:** Verbatim, in full, from `public.bible_verses` (WEB, public domain) via read-only query. Two source rows (Matthew 5:22, James 3:6) carried translator footnotes in curly braces — the underlying words ("Raca," "Gehenna") are displayed exactly as written; their footnote definitions were moved into "A Little Context" rather than left as bracketed text or silently swapped for a different word. Psalm 32:4-5's "Selah" markers were stripped as musical notation, not text. Nothing about the underlying wording of any displayed verse was altered.

**One deliberate content-appropriateness cut, distinct from the "shortest complete passage" rule:** 2 Samuel 12's Scripture is trimmed to verses 1-9 and 13, skipping verses 10-12. Those verses pronounce a specific, sexually explicit consequence (a coming public sexual violation of David's household) that isn't necessary to this dinner's actual subject — confrontation and confession — and isn't appropriate for a family read-aloud. The dinner's complete arc (accusation, conviction, confession, forgiveness pronounced) stands intact without them; verse 14, which is a different and much heavier thread (the child's coming death), was also left out so the dinner stays focused on the confrontation itself rather than opening a second, unrelated grief thread better suited to Batch 04's territory.

### Dinner 1 — Genesis 4:6-7 — "Sin Crouches at the Door"
*New. Theme: anger at the choice point, before it becomes something worse.*

**Scripture:** Yahweh said to Cain, "Why are you angry? Why has the expression of your face fallen? If you do well, will it not be lifted up? If you don't do well, sin crouches at the door. Its desire is for you, but you are to rule over it."

**A Little Context:** Cain is angry because his offering wasn't accepted the way his brother Abel's was. Before anything else happens — and something worse does happen, just a few verses later — God meets Cain right at the anger itself, and asks him a real question instead of condemning him outright.

**For the Table Tonight:** What usually sets you off — is it a big thing, or is it small things stacked up?

**Go Deeper:** When you feel anger rising, what does "ruling over it" actually look like for you in the moment, not just in theory?

**Push Further:** God doesn't tell Cain his anger is the problem — he tells him what he does next is the choice that's still open. What's the difference between anger itself and the door it can open?

**Prayer:** Lord, you met Cain right at his anger, before anything worse happened. Meet us there too, and help us choose well at the door. Amen.

### Dinner 2 — Matthew 5:21-22 — "Anger and the Heart of Murder"
*New. Theme: Jesus locates something serious underneath ordinary anger.*

**Scripture:** "You have heard that it was said to the ancient ones, 'You shall not murder;' and 'Whoever shall murder shall be in danger of the judgment.' But I tell you, that everyone who is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment; and whoever shall say to his brother, 'Raca!' shall be in danger of the council; and whoever shall say, 'You fool!' shall be in danger of the fire of Gehenna.

**A Little Context:** Jesus isn't adding a new law here — he's showing that the command against murder was never only about the final act. He traces the same danger back to contempt: the anger and the insults that come long before anyone would call it violence. "Raca" was an insult in Jesus's own language meaning something like "empty-headed" or "worthless." "Gehenna" was a real valley outside Jerusalem, used by Jesus elsewhere as an image of judgment — both words name the way contempt escalates toward writing someone off entirely.

**For the Table Tonight:** What's an insult, even a small one, that's stuck with you longer than you expected it to?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a difference, in your own life, between how you talk about someone and how you'd talk to them directly?

**Push Further:** Jesus puts unresolved contempt in the same sentence as murder — not the same act, but the same root. What does that suggest about why he cared so much about how we talk about each other, not just what we do to each other?

**Prayer:** Jesus, you saw what's underneath the anger, not just the act itself. Search us there tonight, in the words we'd never call violence. Amen.

### Dinner 3 — Ephesians 4:26-27 — "Don't Let the Sun Go Down"
*New. Theme: anger itself isn't the danger — what we do with it is.*

**Scripture:** "Be angry, and don't sin." Don't let the sun go down on your wrath, neither give place to the devil.

**A Little Context:** Paul doesn't tell believers not to be angry — he assumes they will be, sometimes rightly. What he warns against is what anger becomes when it's left to sit past its own day.

**For the Table Tonight:** Have you ever gone to bed angry at someone and woken up feeling completely different about it — better or worse?

**Go Deeper:** What usually happens when you sit on anger instead of dealing with it — does it fade, or does it grow?

**Push Further:** Paul separates "be angry" from "don't sin" — as if they're not automatically the same thing. What's the actual line between them, in your own experience?

**Prayer:** Father, we're not asking to never feel this. Just help us not let it outlast its own day. Amen.

### Dinner 4 — Proverbs 29:11 — "A Fool Vents All His Anger"
*New. Theme: expressing anger vs. being controlled by it.*

**Scripture:** A fool vents all of his anger, But a wise man brings himself under control.

**A Little Context:** This is a short, plain proverb — not a claim that wisdom means never feeling anger, but that wisdom shows in what you do with all of it in the moment.

**For the Table Tonight:** Do you tend to say everything you're feeling in the moment, or hold most of it back?

**Go Deeper:** Has "venting all of it" ever cost you something afterward that you wish you'd kept back?

**Push Further:** The proverb doesn't say a wise person has no anger — it says a wise person doesn't let all of it out at once. What's worth saying in the moment, and what's worth waiting on?

**Prayer:** God, give us the kind of self-control this proverb is talking about — not silence, but wisdom about what needs to be said now and what can wait. Amen.

### Dinner 5 — Proverbs 18:13 — "Answering Before You've Heard"
*New. Theme: interrupting, arguing to win instead of understand.*

**Scripture:** He who gives answer before he hears, That is folly and shame to him.

**A Little Context:** A one-line proverb about a habit almost everyone recognizes — jumping to a response before actually taking in what the other person said.

**For the Table Tonight:** Are you someone who waits to hear the whole thing, or someone who's already forming your answer halfway through?

**Go Deeper:** Think of a recent disagreement — how much of it did you actually hear, and how much were you just waiting to respond to?

**Push Further:** This proverb calls answering too early "folly and shame" — pretty strong language for something that just feels like normal conversation. Why might genuinely hearing someone out be harder, and more important, than it sounds?

**Prayer:** Lord, slow us down enough to actually hear each other tonight and every night, before we're already forming our answer. Amen.

### Dinner 6 — James 3:5-10 — "Blessing and Cursing From the Same Mouth"
*New. Theme: sarcasm, words that wound, how we talk to the people we love most.*

**Scripture:** So the tongue is also a little member, and boasts great things. See how a small fire can spread to a large forest! And the tongue is a fire. The world of iniquity among our members is the tongue, which defiles the whole body, and sets on fire the course of nature. For every kind of animal, bird, creeping thing, and thing in the sea, is tamed, and has been tamed by mankind. But nobody can tame the tongue. It is a restless evil, full of deadly poison. With it we bless our God and Father, and with it we curse men, who are made in the image of God. Out of the same mouth comes forth blessing and cursing. My brothers, these things ought not to be so.

**A Little Context:** James isn't talking about strangers here — the tension he names is that the very same mouth that prays and worships can turn around and tear someone down, sometimes in the same day, sometimes at the same table.

**For the Table Tonight:** What's a tone of voice — sarcastic, sharp, dismissive — that you know gets under someone else's skin?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a way you talk to the people closest to you that you'd never talk to anyone else?

**Push Further:** James says taming animals is easier than taming the tongue. What makes the words we say to the people we love the hardest ones to actually control?

**Prayer:** God, we don't want the same mouth that praises you to be the one that wounds the people we love. Tame our tongues tonight. Amen.

### Dinner 7 — Proverbs 17:14 — "Starting a Quarrel"
*New. Theme: how small disagreements escalate.*

**Scripture:** The beginning of strife is like breaching a dam, Therefore stop contention before quarreling breaks out.

**A Little Context:** The image is simple and physical — once a dam breaks, the water doesn't stop where you want it to. The proverb's advice is about timing: deal with it before the break, not after.

**For the Table Tonight:** Can you think of a small disagreement that turned into something much bigger than it needed to be?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a moment early in an argument where you can usually feel it about to "breach the dam" — and what do you normally do right then?

**Push Further:** This proverb doesn't say avoid all conflict — it says catch it early. What would it look like, in a real disagreement, to stop it "before quarreling breaks out" instead of after?

**Prayer:** Father, help us notice the moment before things break, not just clean up after they do. Amen.

### Dinner 8 — Matthew 18:15-17 — "If Your Brother Sins Against You"
*New. Theme: going directly to the person, not around them.*

**Scripture:** "If your brother sins against you, go, show him his fault between you and him alone. If he listens to you, you have gained back your brother. But if he doesn't listen, take one or two more with you, that at the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established. If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the assembly. If he refuses to hear the assembly also, let him be to you as a Gentile or a tax collector.

**A Little Context:** Jesus gives a specific order here — go to the person first, privately, before involving anyone else. Each further step only happens if the one before it didn't work.

**For the Table Tonight:** When you're upset with someone, is your first instinct to talk to them directly, or to talk to someone else about it first?

**Go Deeper:** What makes going straight to the person harder than talking about them to someone else instead?

**Push Further:** Jesus starts with "between you and him alone" — not a group text, not mentioning it to someone else first. What does that first step cost that the easier version doesn't?

**Prayer:** Jesus, going directly to someone is harder than talking around them. Give us the courage to go first to the person, not last. Amen.

### Dinner 9 — Philippians 4:2-3 — "I Exhort Euodia... I Exhort Syntyche"
*New. Theme: a real, named conflict between two respected people.*

**Scripture:** I exhort Euodia, and I exhort Syntyche, to think the same way in the Lord. Yes, I beg you also, true partner, help these women, for they labored with me in the Gospel, with Clement also, and the rest of my fellow workers, whose names are in the book of life.

**A Little Context:** Euodia and Syntyche were real leaders in the Philippian church, and whatever their disagreement was, Paul cared enough about it to name them both, directly, in a letter meant to be read aloud to the whole church.

**For the Table Tonight:** Has a disagreement between two people you respect ever surprised you?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a conflict you know about, between other people, that you've stayed out of but wondered whether you should help with?

**Push Further:** Paul doesn't take a side between Euodia and Syntyche — he asks someone else to help them, and asks both of them to agree "in the Lord." What does it look like to care about a conflict's resolution without needing to decide who was right?

**Prayer:** Lord, even people who genuinely love you can still disagree. Help us be the kind of help Paul was asking for, not one more side. Amen.

### Dinner 10 — Acts 15:36-39 — "Paul and Barnabas Disagree"
*New. Theme: a sharp, unresolved disagreement between two good people.*

**Scripture:** After some days Paul said to Barnabas, "Let's return now and visit our brothers in every city in which we proclaimed the word of the Lord, to see how they are doing." Barnabas planned to take John, who was called Mark, with them also. But Paul didn't think that it was a good idea to take with them someone who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia, and didn't go with them to do the work. Then the contention grew so sharp that they separated from each other. Barnabas took Mark with him, and sailed away to Cyprus,

**A Little Context:** Paul and Barnabas were close partners in ministry, and this disagreement was sharp enough that Scripture uses the word "contention" and tells us plainly that they parted ways because of it. The text doesn't say which one of them was right.

**For the Table Tonight:** Have you ever disagreed with someone you genuinely respect, and neither of you backed down?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a relationship in your life that changed shape after a disagreement, even if it didn't end?

**Push Further:** Scripture records this falling-out without telling us who was right, and without erasing either man's importance to the story that follows. What does it mean that two good people, both trying to do the right thing, can still end up on opposite sides?

**Prayer:** Father, not every disagreement gets resolved the way we'd want. Help us trust you with the ones that don't. Amen.

### Dinner 11 — Romans 12:18 — "As Far As It Depends on You"
*New. Theme: peace as a real effort, not something fully in your control.*

**Scripture:** If it is possible, as much as it is up to you, be at peace with all men.

**A Little Context:** Paul's wording is careful — "if it is possible," "as much as it is up to you." He's not commanding a guaranteed outcome, only the part that's actually yours to give.

**For the Table Tonight:** Is there a relationship where you've done what you could, but peace still hasn't come?

**Go Deeper:** What's the difference between giving up on peace and accepting that it isn't only up to you?

**Push Further:** Paul doesn't promise every relationship will end at peace — he only asks for your part of it. What does that free you from, and what does it still ask of you?

**Prayer:** God, we can't make peace happen alone. Help us do our part honestly, and trust you with the rest. Amen.

### Dinner 12 — Romans 12:19-21 — "Overcome Evil With Good"
*New. Theme: not repaying wrong for wrong.*

**Scripture:** Don't seek revenge yourselves, beloved, but give place to God's wrath. For it is written, "Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord." Therefore "If your enemy is hungry, feed him. If he is thirsty, give him a drink. For in doing so, you will heap coals of fire on his head." Don't be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

**A Little Context:** Paul isn't telling his readers that what was done to them didn't matter — he's telling them who gets to settle it, and it isn't them.

**For the Table Tonight:** Have you ever wanted to get back at someone, even just a little, for something they did to you?

**Go Deeper:** What's a small, practical way you could respond to someone difficult with actual good instead of matching them?

**Push Further:** Paul says vengeance belongs to God, not to us — not because the wrong doesn't matter, but because settling it isn't our job. What does it cost to actually let that be true?

**Prayer:** Lord, we don't want to be overcome by whatever's been done to us. Help us answer it with good, and leave the settling to you. Amen.

### Dinner 13 — Leviticus 19:17-18 — "Do Not Bear a Grudge"
*New. Theme: silent resentment vs. honest, direct rebuke.*

**Scripture:** "You shall not hate your brother in your heart. You shall surely rebuke your neighbor, and not bear sin because of him. You shall not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of your people; but you shall love your neighbor as yourself. I am Yahweh.

**A Little Context:** This command pairs two things people usually treat as opposites: don't hate silently, and don't take revenge — instead, say the hard thing directly. Holding it in and lashing out are treated here as two versions of the same failure.

**For the Table Tonight:** Do you tend to say what's bothering you, or hold it in until it comes out some other way?

**Go Deeper:** Is there a grudge you've been carrying quietly instead of actually addressing?

**Push Further:** This command treats silent hatred and open vengeance as connected, not opposite problems — and puts "rebuke your neighbor" in between them as the alternative to both. Why might honest, direct confrontation be the harder, healthier option compared to either one?

**Prayer:** Father, we don't want to carry grudges quietly or get even loudly. Teach us the harder, honest way in between. Amen.

### Dinner 14 — Psalm 32:3-5 — "While I Kept Silent"
*New. Theme: the cost of not confessing, not just to God but often to each other.*

**Scripture:** When I kept silence, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy on me. My strength was sapped in the heat of summer. I acknowledged my sin to you. I didn't hide my iniquity. I said, I will confess my transgressions to Yahweh, And you forgave the iniquity of my sin.

**A Little Context:** David describes what it physically felt like to carry something unconfessed, and then what changed the moment he stopped hiding it and just said it plainly.

**For the Table Tonight:** Have you ever noticed that not admitting something actually takes more energy than just saying it?

**Go Deeper:** Is there something you've been carrying quietly that's wearing on you more than you've let on?

**Push Further:** David describes real physical weight from staying silent, and real relief the moment he confessed. What is it about naming something honestly, instead of managing it privately, that actually changes something?

**Prayer:** God, we carry things quietly that we don't need to carry alone. Give us the courage David found to just say it. Amen.

### Dinner 15 — 2 Samuel 12:1-9, 13 — "You Are the Man"
*New. Theme: confrontation done right, and real repentance in response.*

**Scripture:** Yahweh sent Nathan to David. He came to him, and said to him, "There were two men in one city; the one rich, and the other poor. The rich man had very many flocks and herds, but the poor man had nothing, except one little ewe lamb, which he had bought and raised. It grew up together with him, and with his children. It ate of his own food, drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom, and was to him like a daughter. A traveler came to the rich man, and he spared to take of his own flock and of his own herd, to dress for the wayfaring man who had come to him, but took the poor man's lamb, and dressed it for the man who had come to him." David's anger was greatly kindled against the man, and he said to Nathan, "As Yahweh lives, the man who has done this is worthy to die! He shall restore the lamb fourfold, because he did this thing, and because he had no pity!" Nathan said to David, "You are the man. This is what Yahweh, the God of Israel, says: 'I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you out of the hand of Saul. I gave you your master's house, and your master's wives into your bosom, and gave you the house of Israel and of Judah; and if that would have been too little, I would have added to you many more such things. Why have you despised the word of Yahweh, to do that which is evil in his sight? You have struck Uriah the Hittite with the sword, and have taken his wife to be your wife, and have slain him with the sword of the children of Ammon." David said to Nathan, "I have sinned against Yahweh." Nathan said to David, "Yahweh also has put away your sin. You will not die.

**A Little Context:** Nathan doesn't accuse David directly at first — he tells a story that lets David's own sense of justice condemn the wrongdoer, before revealing that the story was about David himself. David's response is immediate and unguarded: no excuse, no defense, just "I have sinned."

**For the Table Tonight:** Has anyone ever confronted you in a way that actually helped, rather than just making you defensive?

**Go Deeper:** When you've done something wrong, is your first instinct to explain it, or to just own it the way David does here?

**Push Further:** David gets angrier at the story than he ever gets at himself — until Nathan says four words. What does it take to see clearly in ourselves what we can spot instantly in someone else?

**Prayer:** God, give us Nathan's courage to speak the truth, and David's honesty to actually hear it. Amen.

### Dinner 16 — Matthew 5:23-24 — "Leave Your Gift at the Altar"
*New. Theme: apology as the first move, before anything else.*

**Scripture:** "If therefore you are offering your gift at the altar, and there remember that your brother has anything against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and go your way. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift.

**A Little Context:** Jesus is describing someone in the middle of worship — already at the altar — who remembers a relationship that needs attention, and tells them to stop and go deal with it first.

**Go Deeper:** What usually stops you from apologizing first, even when you know you should?

**Prayer:** God, show us who we owe an apology to, and give us the courage to go make it right before we bring you anything else. Amen.

### Dinner 17 — Matthew 18:21-35 — "Seventy Times Seven"
*New. Theme: the scale of forgiveness, and what happens when we forget how much we've been forgiven.*

**Scripture:** Then Peter came and said to him, "Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I don't tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven. Therefore the Kingdom of Heaven is like a certain king, who wanted to reconcile accounts with his servants. When he had begun to reconcile, one was brought to him who owed him ten thousand talents. But because he couldn't pay, his lord commanded him to be sold, with his wife, his children, and all that he had, and payment to be made. The servant therefore fell down and kneeled before him, saying, 'Lord, have patience with me, and I will repay you all!' The lord of that servant, being moved with compassion, released him, and forgave him the debt. "But that servant went out, and found one of his fellow servants, who owed him one hundred denarii, and he grabbed him, and took him by the throat, saying, 'Pay me what you owe!' "So his fellow servant fell down at his feet and begged him, saying, 'Have patience with me, and I will repay you!' He would not, but went and cast him into prison, until he should pay back that which was due. So when his fellow servants saw what was done, they were exceedingly sorry, and came and told to their lord all that was done. Then his lord called him in, and said to him, 'You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt, because you begged me. Shouldn't you also have had mercy on your fellow servant, even as I had mercy on you?' His lord was angry, and delivered him to the tormentors, until he should pay all that was due to him. So my heavenly Father will also do to you, if you don't each forgive your brother from your hearts for his misdeeds."

**A Little Context:** Ten thousand talents was a debt no servant could ever realistically repay — the amount is deliberately, almost comically enormous, set right next to the second servant's very ordinary, repayable debt. Peter's question about counting to seven led Jesus somewhere Peter probably wasn't expecting.

**For the Table Tonight:** Have you ever been forgiven for something and then turned around and been unusually hard on someone else for something small?

**Go Deeper:** Is there someone you're keeping a tally on — counting how many times they've let you down?

**Push Further:** The first debt is impossible to repay; the second is small enough to actually pay back. Why do you think Jesus made the gap between the two debts so extreme?

**Prayer:** Jesus, we've been forgiven more than we can repay. Help that shape how we treat the much smaller debts owed to us. Amen.

### Dinner 18 — 2 Corinthians 5:18-19 — "The Ministry of Reconciliation"
*New. Theme: reconciliation as something bigger than any one relationship.*

**Scripture:** But all things are of God, who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation; namely, that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses, and having committed to us the word of reconciliation.

**A Little Context:** Paul is describing something bigger than any single relationship here — God reconciling an entire world to himself through Christ, and then handing that same work to ordinary people to carry forward.

**For the Table Tonight:** Where have you seen someone actually help two other people find their way back to each other?

**Go Deeper:** Is reconciliation something you think of as your job, or as something that just happens or doesn't?

**Push Further:** Paul calls this "the ministry of reconciliation" — an actual assignment, not just a nice outcome when it happens. What changes if you start treating reconciliation as your job, not just something you hope works out on its own?

**Prayer:** God, you reconciled us to yourself first. Give us a part in that same work between the people around us. Amen.

### Dinner 19 — Philemon 1:15-18 — "Paul's Appeal for Onesimus"

**Scripture:** For perhaps he was therefore separated from you for a while, that you would have him forever, no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother, especially to me, but how much rather to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord. **For the Table Tonight:** Has anyone ever vouched for you or stepped in to help repair a relationship you'd damaged?

**Push Further:** Paul asks, he doesn't command — and he offers to personally absorb the cost of what's owed rather than requiring Philemon to just let it go. What's the difference between asking someone to forgive a debt and offering to pay it yourself?

**Prayer:** Jesus, some of us have needed someone to step in and help repair what we couldn't fix alone. Make us that kind of help for someone else. Amen.

### Dinner 20 — 2 Timothy 4:14-16 — "The Lord Will Repay"
*New. Theme: when reconciliation is difficult or genuinely not possible.*

**Scripture:** Alexander, the coppersmith, did much evil to me. The Lord will repay him according to his works, of whom you also must beware; for he greatly opposed our words. At my first defense, no one came to help me, but all left me. May it not be held against them.

**A Little Context:** Paul names a specific person who genuinely wronged him, and doesn't pretend that relationship is fine — he warns Timothy to be careful of the same man. In the very next breath, he also releases the friends who abandoned him at his lowest moment, without requiring anything from them first.

**For the Table Tonight:** Is there a relationship in your life you've had to accept won't be repaired, at least not right now?

**Go Deeper:** What's the difference, for you, between forgiving someone and deciding you still need to be careful around them?

**Push Further:** Paul does two different things in the same breath here — he warns Timothy about someone who genuinely harmed him, and he releases people who let him down, without either one requiring the relationship to go back to how it was. Why might warning someone and releasing someone both be honest responses, instead of contradictory ones?

**Prayer:** Lord, some relationships aren't repaired, not yet and maybe not ever. Help us release what we're carrying without pretending it's settled when it isn't. Amen.

### Coverage

**Anger (4):** Dinners 1-4 move from anger's origin (Cain, the choice point before it becomes something worse) through Jesus locating contempt as anger's real danger, to Paul's permission to feel it without letting it outlast its day, to a plain proverb about venting versus self-control. Anger is never treated as sin by default anywhere in this batch — each dinner distinguishes having anger from being ruled by it, per the brief.

**Communication / conflict behavior (5):** Dinners 5-7, and 11-12, hit the brief's practical list directly — interrupting and answering before listening (5), sarcasm and words that cut close to home (6), letting small things escalate (7), the pull to retaliate (12) — and Dinner 11 (Romans 12:18) carries the batch's clearest boundary-setting nuance: peace is only ever "as far as it depends on you," not a guaranteed or fully controllable outcome.

**Direct confrontation, done right (2):** Dinner 8 (Jesus's own process, go to the person first) and Dinner 15 (Nathan and David, a real narrative example of it working) are a deliberate pair — one gives the principle, the other shows it happening.

**Real conflict between real, named, respected people (2):** Dinners 9-10 (Euodia and Syntyche; Paul and Barnabas) — both make the point that people who genuinely love God still end up in sharp disagreement, and neither dinner assigns blame to one side.

**Grudges, confession, apology (3):** Dinner 13 (Leviticus, silent hatred vs. honest rebuke), Dinner 14 (the physical cost of staying silent), Dinner 16 (apology as the first move, not an afterthought) — this last one directly carries the brief's "who might need an apology from you" angle rather than only asking who needs to be forgiven.

**Forgiveness's scale and cost (1):** Dinner 17, the unforgiving servant — deliberately placed after the grudge/confession cluster so the parable's warning lands on a table that's just spent several dinners on its own side of the ledger.

**Reconciliation (3):** Dinners 18-20 move from reconciliation as a calling bigger than any one relationship, to a specific, real broken relationship being actively repaired (Onesimus), to reconciliation that Paul explicitly does not pursue (Alexander the coppersmith) — the batch's closing dinner exists specifically so "some relationships remain complicated" isn't just a line in the brief but an actual dinner a family can have.

### Difficult theological decisions

**2 Samuel 12 required a content-appropriateness cut**, detailed in the header — verses 10-12 (sexually explicit consequence language) and verse 14 (the child's death, a separate grief thread) were left out, ending the passage at verse 13's forgiveness pronouncement. This is a different kind of edit than anything in prior batches — not a "shortest complete passage" trim, but a judgment that specific content doesn't belong in a family read-aloud regardless of length concerns.

**Forgiveness and reconciliation are kept visibly separate throughout**, per the brief's core distinction. No dinner implies that forgiving someone requires resuming the relationship on its old terms — Dinner 20 (2 Timothy 4) exists specifically to show Paul forgiving people in the same breath as warning about someone he is still, deliberately, keeping distance from.

**No dinner tells the table who's right in a conflict.** Dinners 9 and 10 both involve real disagreements between named, respected biblical figures, and neither context box nor any question asks the table to decide who was correct — consistent with §7 of the editorial standard (don't tell the table what the tension means).

**"Turn the other cheek" language and passages historically used to pressure people into staying in unsafe situations were deliberately not included.** No dinner in this batch implies that forgiveness requires remaining in danger, or that peace means pretending nothing happened — Romans 12:18's "as far as it depends on you" and 2 Timothy 4:14-16 (warning Timothy to be careful of a specific person) were selected in part because they carry this nuance honestly, without needing a disclaimer bolted onto any other dinner.

### Anything potentially harmful caught and corrected

Two accuracy errors were introduced during first drafting and caught before finalizing, both in Dinner 2 (Matthew 5:21-22): "Raca" was initially replaced with an invented English paraphrase ("You empty-headed fool!") instead of displaying the actual WEB text, and "Gehenna" was initially replaced with "hell." Both are violations of the standing rule that Scripture must be displayed exactly as written — fixed by restoring the verbatim WEB wording and moving the definitions into "A Little Context" instead of substituting words inline. No other passage in this batch required a similar correction.

On the "don't ignite a fight at the table" concern specifically: every question in this batch was written to be answerable in general terms — about anger, conflict, or forgiveness as a pattern in someone's life — rather than pointed at a specific person or specific conflict currently happening at that table. No question asks "who here" or names a directional target ("what does someone at this table need to hear from you"); the closest any dinner comes is Dinner 16's "who might need an apology from you," which stays addressed to the individual's own reflection rather than the group.

2. **Dinner 17 (Matthew 18:21-35, "Seventy Times Seven")** — the debt gap does the batch's hardest emotional work without any added commentary; the parable is simply allowed to be as uncomfortable as it is.
3. **Dinner 20 (2 Timothy 4:14-16, "The Lord Will Repay")** — the rare passage that shows forgiveness and continued caution held together in the same breath, which is exactly what the brief asked this batch to make room for.

### 3 weakest

1. **Dinner 4 (Proverbs 29:11)** — solid but thin; it sits close enough to Dinner 5's territory (self-control in the moment) that it's the dinner most likely to feel like a warm-up rather than a full conversation on its own.
2. **Dinner 18 (2 Corinthians 5:18-19)** — the theology is right and the "ministry of reconciliation" framing is genuinely useful, but the passage itself is more abstract than most of this batch, and the questions have to do more work to make it concrete than the narrative-driven dinners around it.
3. **Dinner 9 (Philippians 4:2-3)** — a good idea (a real, named conflict between two respected leaders) constrained by how little the text actually says about what the disagreement even was; the dinner is honest about that limit rather than inventing detail to fill it, but it's the thinnest Scripture base in the batch.

### Editorial lesson

One is worth naming, though it's a correction to how I was already applying an existing rule rather than a new rule: §3 of the editorial standard requires Scripture to be displayed "in full, exactly as written," and this batch is the first time footnoted words themselves (not just the footnote bracket) got silently swapped for a paraphrase during drafting, in Matthew 5:21-22. The existing rule already covered this — the mistake was in execution, not in a gap in the standard — so no new rule is being added, but it's flagged here as a concrete example worth remembering: stripping a footnote means removing the bracketed note, never rewording the word or phrase the footnote was attached to.
